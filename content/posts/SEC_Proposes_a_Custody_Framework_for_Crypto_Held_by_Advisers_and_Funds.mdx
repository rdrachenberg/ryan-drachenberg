On October 1, the SEC announced a proposal on how registered investment advisers and regulated funds can custody crypto assets under the federal securities laws. It is a proposal, not a final rule, but it speaks directly to a question that has shaped how institutional crypto products get built: who is allowed to hold the keys?

## What the SEC proposed

According to SEC press release 2026-100, the agency proposed rules and amendments creating a tailored custody framework for registered investment advisers and for regulated funds, meaning registered investment companies and business development companies. The changes sit under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and the Investment Company Act of 1940.

The release says the goals are to modernize custody rules, expand investor choice, and remove barriers to crypto-related investment advice. It also says the proposal would let regulated funds offer strategies tied to a wider range of crypto assets.

The key provisions listed by the SEC are:

- Updates to financial statement audit requirements for registered investment advisers.
- Updates to broker-dealer custodial service requirements for regulated funds.
- Permission for crypto assets to be held in self-custody under certain circumstances.
- Permission to use state trust companies as custodians for client and regulated fund crypto assets.

SEC Chairman Paul S. Atkins said the proposal would give advisers and funds "a compliant pathway where none existed before." Public comments are open for 60 days after the proposing release is published in the Federal Register.

## Why it matters

Custody is where traditional securities rules and crypto's key-based ownership collide. Existing custody requirements were written around qualified custodians and assets that sit in an account. Naming state trust companies and conditional self-custody as acceptable arrangements widens the set of infrastructure providers an adviser could lawfully use.

The timing also matters. As The Block reported on September 16, the Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act on a 49 to 50 procedural vote, and analysts at Bernstein said they expected the SEC and CFTC to pursue specific rulemaking in an aggressive and swift manner. The custody proposal is one example of agencies acting without a new statute.

## What builders should take from it

We would treat this as a signal about demand, not a finished rulebook. A few practical points:

- **Custody tech may get more customers.** If the proposal is adopted, advisers and funds would need custodians, wallet infrastructure and audit tooling that fit the final conditions.
- **Details decide everything.** The SEC release says self-custody would be allowed only under certain circumstances. The conditions in the full proposed rule will determine whether that is usable for real products.
- **Comment letters shape outcomes.** Teams that build custody, signing or attestation systems can submit technical feedback during the 60-day window.
- **Nothing changes yet.** Until a final rule is adopted, existing custody obligations continue to apply.
