On October 21 the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency announced the final version of its rule implementing the GENIUS Act, the federal law governing payment stablecoins. The proposal landed in February; this is the version issuers will actually have to build against, and it takes effect on the same day as the statute itself.

## What the OCC announced

According to OCC News Release 2026-92 and Bulletin 2026-50, the rule sets requirements for permitted payment stablecoin issuers and foreign payment stablecoin issuers under OCC jurisdiction, along with certain custody activities by OCC-supervised entities. It covers everything the OCC must write under the Act except Bank Secrecy Act, anti-money laundering and OFAC sanctions requirements, which will come in a separate rulemaking coordinated with the Treasury Department.

Most of the text lives in a new 12 CFR Part 15. The bulletin lists its subjects:

- Permitted activities and reserve assets
- Redemption and risk management
- Audits, reports and supervision
- Transition of state qualified issuers to the federal framework
- Custody, applications and registrations
- Examination of foreign issuers
- Revocation of approval, plus capital and an operational backstop

The OCC also made conforming changes to its capital adequacy, prompt corrective action, fee and rules-of-practice parts. The rule and the Act both take effect on January 18, 2027.

## What changed from the proposal

The OCC's preamble says the agency made modifications in response to comments, which came from crypto firms, community bankers, trade groups and academics. Several are worth a builder's attention:

- **Reserve management is more flexible.** The OCC says the changes give issuers more latitude while keeping core liquidity, diversification and concentration requirements.
- **Risk management scales.** Section 15.13 sets standards that scale with the nature, scope and risk of an issuer's activities, and the OCC says supervision focuses on activities over institution type.
- **Capital is simplified.** The final rule includes capital and operational backstop requirements that are more detailed than the proposal but, in the OCC's words, consistent with it, with the capital elements simplified.
- **Issuer structures.** The preamble says changes accommodate certain structures, including special purpose vehicle issuers designed to protect holders' rights.

The preamble also records the debate. Commenters split on how prescriptive reserve rules should be, argued over the proposal's rebuttable presumption on yield paid through affiliates or third parties, and objected to provisions that could freeze or extend redemption during a spike in requests, arguing they could accelerate a run. The preamble describes these arguments; we are not drawing conclusions here about how each was resolved beyond what the release states.

## Waivers and timing

Bulletin 2026-51 covers waivers and application guidance, and Bulletin 2026-52 covers revised reporting forms. Both depend on Office of Management and Budget review of the associated information collections. The OCC says it will announce separately when it starts accepting applications and waiver requests. The Comptroller, Jonathan V. Gould, described the rule as a framework with "strong standards for reserve backing, redemption, and risk management."

## What builders should take from it

If you build payments, wallets or treasury tooling around stablecoins, a few practical points follow:

- **Plan for January 18, 2027.** That is the date the rule and the Act take effect, and the OCC has not yet opened its application window.
- **Watch the AML piece.** Compliance obligations that touch transaction monitoring and sanctions screening are still pending in the separate rulemaking.
- **Read Part 15 directly.** Redemption, reserve and custody provisions will shape what integrations with a regulated issuer can promise users.
- **Expect other agencies.** The OCC says it will coordinate with other agencies implementing parts of the Act, so state and other federal regimes may differ in detail.

This is a regulatory summary, not legal advice, and issuers should read the rule text itself.
